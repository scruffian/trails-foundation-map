# Supporting grassroots dirt jumping in the UK

*A proposal for a national charitable organisation — working draft*

## The problem: a sport without a safety net

Dirt jumping has an active and dedicated community across the UK. Riders — predominantly young people — build and maintain spots through extraordinary voluntary effort, developing real skills in construction, terrain reading, and community organisation. Yet despite this dedication, the vast majority of these spots are informal, precarious, and eventually destroyed.

The pattern repeats constantly: a group of riders identifies land, builds jumps over weeks or months, establishes a community, and is then shut down — not because the activity is harmful, but because it lacks the formal structures that would make it legitimate. The riders start again elsewhere, and the cycle continues. Years of skilled labour are lost. Communities are scattered.

This is not a problem of enthusiasm or skill. It is a problem of institutional support. The riders are there. The demand is there. What is missing is the organisational infrastructure that would allow these spots to become permanent.

Because the vast majority of dirt jumping takes place informally and outside any official registration or recording system, the true scale of participation has never been measured. That invisibility is itself part of the problem — and one of the things a national organisation could begin to address.

> Research by Thames Valley Police has shown that provision of BMX and dirt jumping facilities for young people typically reduces antisocial behaviour in the surrounding area. Less than 20% of injuries to BMX riders occur on designated facilities — meaning formal spots are significantly safer than informal ones.

## The gap: no organisation exists to fill this role

British Cycling's BMX Freestyle Commission focuses on Olympic disciplines and elite athlete pathways. Cycling UK supports recreational and transport cycling. Neither body has a specific mandate to help grassroots dirt jump spots become permanent, publicly accessible facilities.

Individual success stories — such as Burlish Bike Park in Worcestershire, which used crowdfunding, charitable support, and Sport England funding to formalise a spot with over thirty years of community history — demonstrate that the model works. But each of these projects had to build their knowledge from scratch. There is no shared playbook, no central body to turn to, and no mechanism for successful approaches to be replicated elsewhere.

Where good facilities do exist, they attract riders from significant distances — and those riders are willing to pay for access. This demonstrates real, sustained demand that is not being met by publicly accessible provision.

## The proposal: a national charity for grassroots dirt jumping

We propose the establishment of a Charitable Incorporated Organisation (CIO) with a clear and focused mission: to help grassroots dirt jump spots become permanent, publicly accessible facilities, and to support the people who build and maintain them.

The organisation would not seek to govern the sport, run competitions, or pursue elite performance pathways. Its purpose would be to provide the infrastructure that local clubs and communities need in order to survive and thrive.

### What it would do

- **Act as a central funding body** — receiving grants from Sport England, the National Lottery, and charitable trusts, and redistributing them to affiliated local clubs through a transparent small grants programme
- **Provide a shared knowledge base** — model constitutions, lease agreement templates, planning guidance, and case studies, so clubs do not have to rediscover what others have already learned
- **Carry institutional credibility** — giving local clubs a national body to point to when approaching councils and landowners, and providing the public liability insurance that makes those conversations possible
- **Support paid maintenance roles** — helping facilities fund the skilled people who keep jumps safe and rideable, framing these as genuine employment and skills development opportunities
- **Map the scene nationally** — building a picture of where facilities exist, where demand is unmet, and where investment would have the greatest impact

## Why now: the conditions are right

Interest in dirt jumping and bike park riding is growing. Sport England has a stated strategic priority around reaching young people — particularly young men — who are not engaged by traditional sport. Dirt jumping reaches exactly this demographic, in exactly the informal community settings that funders struggle to access through conventional sports organisations.

There is proven willingness to pay for quality facilities: riders regularly travel significant distances and pay day or annual membership fees to access good spots. This suggests that well-supported facilities can move toward financial sustainability over time, reducing long-term dependence on grant funding.

The combination of clear community demand, a recurring pattern of facility loss, and an absence of formal organisational support represents a strong case for intervention — and a compelling basis for funding applications.

## Next steps

- Engage with the current dirt jumping community to test appetite for this kind of organisation and identify potential co-founders with community credibility
- Map existing clubs and facilities to establish the scale of current provision and unmet need
- Establish formal contact with British Cycling and Sport England to understand what support and recognition might be available
- Constitute formally as a CIO with a broad, representative founding committee
- Begin building relationships with funders ahead of formal grant applications
